Why I self-host my own AI

July 4, 2026 · Arijit Roy

There is a certain irony in my situation. By day I test GPUs for a living — validating unreleased silicon at AMD and measuring exactly what it can do. By night I am the one wrestling with that silicon in the corner of my house. The industry pushes the narrative that AI is a utility you simply subscribe to, but I found myself increasingly uncomfortable with my thoughts and data traversing public networks to reach a black box. So I built my own.

The heart of the operation is a single AMD EPYC 7742 server — 64 cores and 94 GB of RAM. It was never intended for a home environment, but it sits here humming quietly, handling the heavy lifting. The real challenge lies in the graphics cards: three Radeon RX 6700 XTs, roughly 30 GB of VRAM between them. These are consumer cards, not enterprise hardware, and getting them to serve models together takes a bit of elbow grease with ROCm. There is a specific kind of satisfaction in making gaming GPUs do datacentre work, even when it means spending more time debugging drivers than writing prompts.

The software stack is built for stability and isolation. Open-weight LLMs are served by Ollama, with Open WebUI as the chat front-end. Everything lives in Docker Compose stacks, so when something breaks I can spin it back up without losing state. To reach any of it from the internet without opening ports on my router, everything sits behind Caddy and a Cloudflare Tunnel; when I need remote access myself, Tailscale gives me a private mesh instead of an exposed service.

Storage and recovery are non-negotiable. MinIO keeps datasets and model weights local as S3-compatible object storage, and restic pushes encrypted, versioned backups offsite. If the house burns down, the data survives — and stays encrypted until I choose to restore it.

The primary driver is privacy. When I chat with my local models, those conversations never leave the building. No telemetry, no training on my data, no third-party retention. That sovereignty is worth the electricity bill.

There are trade-offs, of course. The hardware and power costs are real compared to a monthly API subscription, and the time investment is higher still — I am not just the user, I am the sysadmin, the network engineer, and the support ticket. But the learning curve is the point. Operating real infrastructure teaches you how the pieces fit together in a way that clicking a button in someone else's dashboard never will. It is messy, occasionally frustrating, and entirely mine.

In a world where AI is becoming increasingly centralized, there is a quiet rebellion in keeping the compute local. I don't do it to be efficient. I do it to understand the machine I am asking to think for me.
